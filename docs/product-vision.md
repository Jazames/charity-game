# Product Vision

## Premise

The player has been appointed to allocate the entire $100 billion principal of
a fictionalized religious institution's endowment. The institution's leaders
want the money to make the world materially better, but they also expect the
player to protect the institution from scandal and reputational damage.

Each morning, the player receives the normal newspaper coverage generated by
the latest apportionments. Internal memos and newspaper extras are exceptional:
they appear only when something notable happens. A warning memo will usually
arrive one day before the associated public extra, although leadership may also
send an occasional “good job” memo. The player then reviews charities, company
investments, and real-estate developments before committing another portion of
the endowment.

The player rarely has complete information. Large charitable gifts can exceed
an organization's ability to use funds responsibly. Incentive programs can
produce cobra effects in which a measured problem appears to improve while the
underlying situation becomes worse. Investments and developments can produce
durable benefits while attracting criticism for increasing the institution's
wealth. Positive press is not necessarily evidence of positive impact, and
negative press is not necessarily evidence of wrongdoing.

## Design pillars

### Legible moral differences without a universal category rule

Some charities are genuinely effective, some companies are intrinsically
harmful, and most companies are morally ordinary. Category alone does not
determine value. A strong charity builds durable local capacity—for example,
constructing a hospital while training and employing local staff—rather than
creating permanent dependence on outside operators. Casinos and manufacturers
whose business depends on addictive or destructive products can be authored as
bad investments without requiring a balancing upside.

### Information arrives through institutions

The simulation is presented through newspapers, internal memoranda, dossiers,
audits, phone calls, and ledgers. The player learns what happened through the
interests and limitations of the organization reporting it.

### Delayed but deterministic feedback

An allocation can generate favorable news immediately, operational warnings a
week later, and measurable social benefits months later. A strong decision can
have a visible cost; a harmful decision can initially look successful.

The game contains no random resolution. Hidden outcomes and thresholds create
uncertainty for the player, but a given sequence of allocations always produces
the same consequences.

### The budget is physical

The remaining corpus should always feel present. Committing billions is an
irreversible act expressed through the ledger and final authorization stamp,
not an abstract skill-tree purchase.

### Satire with systemic targets

The satire should focus on incentives, institutional behavior, media framing,
philanthropic paternalism, financial opacity, and bureaucratic self-protection.
It should not depend on simply declaring one real-world group uniquely evil or
incompetent.

## Player motivations and competing pressures

The player balances several independently scored concerns:

- Actual long-term social benefit.
- Remaining financial and operational resilience.
- Confidence of institutional leadership.
- Public and member sentiment.
- Fraud, investigation, and scandal exposure.
- Fairness across populations and causes.
- Completion of the mandate to allocate the original principal.

These measures should not collapse into a single visible morality score.

## Campaign structure

The recommended campaign is a finite sequence of working days. Each day has a
morning review, an investigation and allocation phase, and an irreversible
submission. Major narrative arcs can introduce deadlines or exceptional
decisions without changing that underlying rhythm.

The campaign ends when all of the original principal is irrevocably committed.
Investments are permanent holdings: the institution does not sell them during
the campaign. Committing capital therefore always reduces the unallocated
principal. A holding can become more or less valuable, but that valuation never
returns cash to the allocation pool.

The player is fired immediately if public relations becomes unacceptable. A
visibly worsening world also damages public relations, so optimizing press
coverage while causing broad harm is not a stable strategy. If the player keeps
the job long enough to allocate the principal, the ending evaluates several
dimensions separately and selects authored epilogues.

## Opportunity families

### Capacity-building charities

Good charities address an immediate need while transferring skills,
infrastructure, and authority to the affected community. Their successful state
should become less dependent on the charity over time. They can still be
overfunded beyond their responsible capacity.

### Perverse-incentive or weak-governance charities

Other charities reward the wrong proxy, maintain dependence, or cannot absorb a
large gift. These can produce cobra effects, waste, or fraud at authored and
deterministic thresholds.

### Ordinary companies

Most businesses are neutral investments. They may create jobs, products, and
returns while also causing normal commercial externalities. Press coverage can
object to the institution earning money without the simulation declaring the
business itself evil.

### Harmful companies

Some businesses, including casinos and manufacturers dependent on addictive
drugs, create direct and predictable harm. Their profitability does not cancel
that harm.

### Speculative companies

Long-shot ventures such as fusion-power companies initially receive neutral
coverage. Their result is hidden from the player but fixed by authored trigger
conditions. If the venture fails, a later extra condemns the wasted money. If
it succeeds, a later extra criticizes how much money the institution made.

## Editorial and legal framing

The project should use a fictional institution, fictional newspaper, fictional
organizations, and original article text. It can clearly draw thematic
inspiration from real institutions without presenting invented conduct as fact.

Real news articles should not be copied or lightly rewritten. If real events
inspire a scenario, the resulting organization, characters, and language should
be independently authored. Names, seals, logos, and trade dress associated with
real organizations should not be used without deliberate legal review.
